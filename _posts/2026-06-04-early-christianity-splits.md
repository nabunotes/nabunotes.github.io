Christianity went from an illegal sect to the Roman state religion in 300 years — then immediately began fracturing over what it actually believed.

## Roman Acceptance

- **313** — Edict of Milan: Constantine legalizes Christianity
- **380** — Edict of Thessalonica: Christianity becomes the official Roman state religion under Theodosius I

By the time the major theological councils began, Christianity was already entrenched as imperial orthodoxy. This made the stakes of each dispute enormous — the losing side wasn't just theologically wrong, it was politically marginalized.

## The Splits

| Year | Event | Result |
|------|-------|--------|
| 325 | Council of Nicaea | Arianism condemned; Trinity defined |
| 431 | Council of Ephesus | Nestorianism condemned; Church of the East breaks away |
| 451 | Council of Chalcedon | Oriental Orthodox breaks away |
| 1054 | Great Schism | Catholic and Eastern Orthodox split |
| 1517 | Protestant Reformation | Luther breaks from Rome |

## Arianism: The First Great Controversy

The question: **Is Jesus fully God, or a created being subordinate to God?**

**Arius** (Alexandria) argued the Son was the first and greatest creation — not co-eternal with the Father. "There was a time when the Son was not." His logic: God is absolutely singular; sharing divine essence with another being undermines monotheism.

**Athanasius** (also Alexandria) countered with *homoousios* — the Son is "of the same substance" as the Father, fully God and co-eternal. His key argument: only a fully divine savior can actually redeem humanity. A demigod can't bridge the gap between God and creation.

Nicaea (325) condemned Arianism, but the controversy lasted another 50 years — several emperors *after* Constantine favored Arianism, and Arian missionaries converted many Germanic tribes. The Nicene position ultimately prevailed through a combination of:

- **Athanasius's persistence** — exiled five times, never yielded
- **The Cappadocian Fathers** — refined the theology: one *ousia* (essence), three *hypostases* (persons)
- **Emperor Theodosius I** — a committed Nicene Christian who convened Constantinople (381) and made Nicene Christianity the state religion

## Chalcedon: How Do Two Natures Coexist?

With the Trinity settled, the next question: **How do Christ's divine and human natures relate?**

Three positions emerged:

- **Nestorianism** — two natures, effectively two persons loosely united. Mary bore the human Jesus, not God. Condemned at Ephesus (431); the Church of the East broke away and spread Christianity into Persia, Central Asia, India, and China.

- **Eutychianism** — opposite extreme: humanity dissolved into divinity like a drop in the ocean. One nature, essentially only divine. Condemned at Chalcedon.

- **Chalcedonian definition** — two natures (divine and human), complete and distinct, yet united in one person. The four key adverbs: *unconfused, unchanged, undivided, unseparated*. Adopted by what became Catholic and Eastern Orthodox.

- **Miaphysitism** (Oriental Orthodox) — one united nature that is *both* divine and human, inseparably joined. They rejected Chalcedon as secretly Nestorian. This position was held by the Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, and Ethiopian churches.

Much of the split may have been **linguistic rather than genuinely theological** — modern ecumenical dialogue has largely concluded both sides were saying the same thing in different philosophical vocabularies.

## Consequences

**Oriental Orthodox (split 451):**
- Preserved ancient liturgical traditions — Coptic, Armenian, Syriac rites are among the oldest in Christianity
- Suffered under Byzantine persecution for rejecting Chalcedon, which may have weakened resistance to the Arab/Islamic conquests of the 7th century
- Survived as minorities under Muslim rule for 1,400 years

**Church of the East (split 431):**
- Thrived under Persian rule (enemies of Byzantium welcomed them)
- Spread Christianity further east than any other branch — reaching Tang Dynasty China and Mongol courts
- Nearly destroyed by Timur's conquests in the 14th century; survives today as the Assyrian Church of the East

**The remaining Chalcedonian church** then split again in 1054 over papal authority and the *filioque* clause, producing Catholic and Eastern Orthodox — and the Catholic church further fractured in 1517 with the Reformation.

## The Pattern

Each split was driven by a mix of genuine theology, philosophical language differences, political power, and ethnic/regional identity. The councils didn't resolve disputes so much as formalize which side had imperial backing.
